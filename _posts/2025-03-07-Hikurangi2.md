At 03:40 we departed in the black of night armed with torches, layers and a plentiful supply of cereal bars to reach the summit in time for sunrise; 700 m to ascend.

All was going well, we even stopped to appreciate the stars, until we began to approach the beginning of 'The Chute': a steep, un-marked section of scree track making up the final 200m ascent to the summit. At 05:20 we began our climb, sticking to the left after the last pole - as instructed - with a wet mist of cloud reducing our visibility to around
25m, we were eager to get going before it became any worse. The wind picked up for a short while so we took shelter behind a friendly boulder. The next 3 hours consisted of the sketchiest scrambling I have ever endured in my life, up and down. We had expected it to be tough but we didn't even reach the summit, the path became more of a rock climbing expedition that we did not have the means to be on, so we turned back; sunrise missed.

As it transpired, we had in fact attempted to climb a landslide, not 'The Chute'. This information made itself readily available as we re-approached the 'last' pole from above: there were infact 4 further poles, with the real last pole less than 100m away! A combination of darkness, cloud and a small and unhelpful ridge had obscured all the poles from our view 3 hours before. Oh well, we'll just have to give it another go, another time!

The walk back to the hut, in daylight and improved conditions, somewhat made up for our mishap: incredible views from sea to land to sea, a dwarf forest and walking downhill boosted our morale even as fatigue began to set in.

Back at the hut we had an early lunch, packed-up, and left our own graffiti advising others on how to
discern the final pole (it had its own warning sign). We set off again at 11:50 for the hike back down to the start; the peak still shrouded in a nebulous mass. About fifteen minutes in we stopped at a collection of sculptures dedicated to Maui, a man who fished the North Island out of the sea, erected 1000m up the mountain. Three, and a bit, hours later we arrived back to the start relieved, the peak now clearly
visible in a blue, hot sky, we got so close!

With Hikurangi in the rear view mirror we set off for Gisborne, about 150 km away. Tolaga Bay was on the way, home to the longest pier in the southern hemisphere: 660m long!.
Time's winged chariot was on our heels as we pressed on towards Gisborne for a place to stay and dinner - our hunger was fortunately subdued by some incredible beach views for the last 20 minutes of the journey.

Thai Surprise, our choice of restaurant for our well-earned dinner. A warming Penang curry along with a sizzling black pepper beef dish thoroughly enjoyed, we dragged our feet back to the van and promptly fell asleep.
